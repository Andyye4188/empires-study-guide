World History Study Guide: Empires & Global Connections

Units 1–2 · Detailed review · English explanations with Chinese support

Based on your 8 downloaded PowerPoints (135 slides) and the teacher’s 162 Quizlet entries. The main chapters explain the material by empire or society. The glossary at the end covers every Quizlet entry, including repeated concepts and corrected spellings. Sections on Portugal, Britain, France, Japan, and other Indigenous societies are shorter because they come mainly from Quizlet rather than a dedicated downloaded PPT.

How to use this guide

  1. For each empire, learn where it was, how rulers controlled it, how it gained wealth, how religion worked, and what weakened it.
  2. Turn each example into an explanation: policy → effect → why that effect strengthened or weakened the state.
  3. Use the comparison tables and practice questions after learning the individual chapters.
  4. Check the correction section before memorizing dates or copying Quizlet definitions.

1. Songhai Empire 桑海帝国

Location: West Africa, especially the Niger River region. Main period in your PPT: c. 1464–1591. Capital: Gao. Other important cities: Timbuktu and Djenné.

Rise and government

  • Sunni Ali expanded Songhai through conquest and gained control of important commercial cities. Control of towns and trade routes connected military power with revenue.
  • Askia Muhammad / Askia the Great (r. 1493–1528) expanded the empire, organized provinces, and strengthened administration. Provincial officials helped the ruler govern a large territory.
  • The emperor controlled officials, military leaders, and nobles. The army protected trade routes, allowing the government to collect taxes more reliably.

Trade, geography, and technology

The Sahara separated regions with different resources. Merchants exchanged West African gold and Saharan salt, creating demand for long-distance trade. The Niger River supported settlement, farming, and regional transport.

Caravans, camels, and improved camel saddles made desert transport more effective. Camels carried substantial loads in arid conditions. Avoid memorizing the PPT’s extreme claim about a fixed number of days without water: endurance depends on conditions.

Trade wealth supported cities, education, and the government. Merchants had high status because their work generated wealth. The government’s protection of trade and its taxation of trade reinforced each other.

Religion, culture, and social hierarchy

  • The ruling elite practiced Islam. Islamic connections linked Songhai with merchants and scholars elsewhere in the Muslim world.
  • Askia’s hajj helped demonstrate his Muslim identity and build diplomatic relationships.
  • Timbuktu became a center of Islamic scholarship. Mosques and madrassas supported learning.
  • Islamic practices coexisted with local traditions. Religious change did not erase every older belief.
  • Emperors and elites stood above commoners and enslaved people. Enslaved people worked in agriculture, mining, households, and transportation. Many were war captives. Some gained freedom through manumission, but this did not end the system of slavery.

Decline

Internal conflict and civil war weakened unity. Ahmad al-Mansur, Morocco’s sultan, sought greater control over wealth and trade. Moroccan troops defeated Songhai at the Battle of Tondibi in 1591, using firearms against forces that relied more heavily on traditional weapons. Loss of major cities and authority over trade contributed to fragmentation.

Causal explanation: Internal division reduced Songhai’s ability to resist invasion, while Morocco’s military advantage helped turn that vulnerability into defeat.

Ready-to-use answer: “Songhai gained power by controlling trans-Saharan trade and using Islam to connect its rulers with wider commercial and scholarly networks. Its decline resulted from internal political conflict combined with a Moroccan invasion supported by gunpowder weapons.”

Remember: Gao = capital; Timbuktu = trade and learning; Askia = administration and Islam; 1591 = Moroccan defeat of Songhai.

PPT evidence: Songhai Empire, slides 6–15 and 21–23.

2. Ottoman Empire 奥斯曼帝国

Location: Originated in Anatolia, then expanded across southeastern Europe, western Asia, and North Africa. Founder in Quizlet: Osman Bey. Major capital: Constantinople/Istanbul.

Expansion and rulers

  • Osman Bey: founder associated with the early Ottoman state.
  • Mehmed II / Mehmet the Conqueror: captured Constantinople in 1453. Cannons helped break the city’s defenses. Orban was a cannon-maker associated with the siege.
  • Selim I / Selim the Grim: expanded Ottoman rule into the Arab lands, including Egypt, and brought the holy cities under Ottoman authority.
  • Suleiman the Magnificent / the Lawgiver: associated with expansion, legal organization, and imperial strength.

How the government controlled a diverse empire

Institution What it did Why it mattered
Sultan Supreme political ruler Directed government, military policy, and imperial resources
Grand vizier Senior minister under the sultan Helped administer a large empire
Devshirme Coercive recruitment of some Christian boys, followed by conversion and training Supplied servants and soldiers tied to the central state
Janissaries Elite soldiers, historically closely connected to devshirme recruitment Strengthened the sultan’s army
Millet arrangements Allowed recognized non-Muslim religious communities considerable internal religious and legal autonomy Helped govern diversity through communal leaders
Jizya Tax imposed on eligible non-Muslim subjects Generated revenue and marked unequal legal status
Tax farming A collector paid the state for collection rights, then collected local revenues Provided state income but could encourage excessive extraction

Devshirme nuance: It created opportunities for some recruits to gain high office, but recruitment and conversion were coercive. The PPT’s focus on Armenian boys is too narrow: Christian communities in the Balkans were especially important.

Religious toleration and its limits

The empire was predominantly governed through Sunni Islamic institutions. Christian and Jewish communities could preserve important religious practices under Ottoman rule. Sephardic Jews, expelled from Iberia, found refuge in Ottoman territories.

However, toleration did not mean equal rights. Jizya and other restrictions reflected the privileged position of Islam. A strong answer explains both the practical benefits of toleration and the inequalities it maintained.

Economy and culture

The empire occupied major routes linking Europe and Asia, including Mediterranean and overland networks. Goods included silk, porcelain, spices, and coffee. Trade and taxation supported the state.

Architecture displayed imperial authority. Topkapi Palace, the Süleymaniye Mosque, and the conversion of Hagia Sophia into a mosque linked political power with cultural and religious authority.

Pressures on stability

Your PPT emphasizes succession conflict and the imprisonment or killing of potential rivals. These could protect a reigning sultan while also disrupting succession. The deck does not give a complete account of the empire’s eventual fall, so do not treat this as a sufficient explanation for centuries of later history.

Ready-to-use answer: “The Ottomans combined military centralization with negotiated religious autonomy. Janissaries strengthened the sultan’s power, while millet arrangements helped govern Christian and Jewish communities. These policies supported stability but preserved religious inequality and included coercive practices.”

PPT evidence: Ottoman Empire Notes, slides 4–17 and 19.

3. Safavid Empire 萨法维帝国

Location: Centered in Persia, especially present-day Iran. Major cities: Tabriz and later Isfahan. Main identity: Shiʿa Islam.

Rise and leadership

  • Ismail I founded the Safavid state in 1501 with support from the Qizilbash, followers known for their red headgear.
  • Making Shiʿa Islam the state religion helped create a shared political identity and distinguish the empire from its Sunni Ottoman rival.
  • Religious enforcement also alienated Sunni subjects and contributed to conflict.
  • Shah Abbas I / Abbas the Great strengthened royal power, reorganized military forces, promoted trade, and developed Isfahan as a major capital.
  • Ghulams were military and administrative servants often recruited from Caucasian populations. Their service could reduce the ruler’s dependence on competing tribal elites.

Ottoman–Safavid rivalry

The rivalry combined religion, territory, trade routes, and political power.

At the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514, Ottoman forces under Selim I defeated Ismail’s army. Ottoman artillery and firearms provided an important advantage over Safavid cavalry. The victory limited Safavid expansion and exposed a military weakness, but it did not instantly settle every later border dispute.

Ottoman embargoes against Safavid silk trade also used economic pressure as a political weapon.

Economy, society, and culture

Trade routes and silk exports contributed to Safavid wealth. European weapons and military advice helped later military development. Persian carpets, textiles, painting, and metalwork supported a reputation for artistic achievement.

Isfahan displayed the wealth and authority of the shah. Women faced restrictions, but Islamic law recognized some rights involving inheritance and divorce. Avoid assuming that legal rights meant equal social power.

The PPT describes limited naval strength and geographical vulnerability. Treat these as constraints on strategy rather than a claim that Persia had no mountains or natural defenses.

Conflict with the Mughals

The Mughal–Safavid War of 1649–1653 concerned Kandahar and control of strategically valuable territory and routes. It is a useful example of a conflict in which territorial and commercial interests mattered even though both states had Muslim rulers.

Decline

Heavy expenditure, weakening revenue, political disorder, and religious tensions undermined the state. Afghan forces captured Isfahan in 1722, causing a major collapse of Safavid authority. Ottoman and Russian expansion added pressure.

Important correction: Nadir Shah displaced the Safavids and founded the Afsharid dynasty in 1736. He was not the “last Safavid king.” See the corrections section for verification.

Ready-to-use answer: “Shiʿa Islam strengthened Safavid identity and royal legitimacy, but enforcing religious uniformity also generated opposition. Competition with the Ottomans involved both sectarian rivalry and control of territory and trade.”

PPT evidence: Safavid Empire Notes, slides 2–9.

4. Mughal Empire 莫卧儿帝国

Location: Indian subcontinent. Main issue: How could Muslim rulers govern a religiously diverse population with a large Hindu majority?

Key rulers

Ruler Main association Historical significance
Babur Victory at Panipat in 1526 Established Mughal rule in northern India
Akbar Toleration, cooperation with Hindu elites, administrative consolidation Broadened support for imperial rule
Shah Jahan Taj Mahal and court splendor Used architecture and luxury to display imperial wealth
Aurangzeb Expansion, restoration of jizya, stricter religious policy Increased territorial reach while creating serious financial and political pressures

Akbar’s government and religious policy

Akbar recruited Hindu elites, including Rajputs, into government and formed political marriage alliances. He abolished jizya and supported religious discussion. These measures helped reduce opposition and connect the central state with local power holders.

The PPT presents Din-i Ilahi as religious syncretism. It was a small court-centered spiritual movement associated with Akbar, rather than a religion adopted by most of the population.

Mansabdars and zamindars

  • Mansabdars: imperial officials and military officers holding ranks assigned by the emperor. Rank related to service obligations and status. Many received revenue assignments called jagirs, which were not permanent private ownership of the assigned territory.
  • Zamindars: local landholders or intermediaries who collected agricultural revenue and exercised local authority. Many had hereditary connections to their districts.

Why this matters: Mansabdars linked military and administrative service to the emperor. Zamindars connected the imperial state with rural communities. They were not interchangeable job titles.

Social structure and economy

Caste distinctions influenced status, occupation, and opportunity. The PPT lists Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras, with Dalits excluded from that four-varna framework. This is a simplified overview of a much more complex society.

Agriculture and land taxes supported the state. Roads and currency helped exchange. India exported textiles and other valuable goods through Indian Ocean networks in return for precious metals and other imports.

Trade evaluation: The empire benefited from maritime trade without exercising complete control over the whole Indian Ocean. Inland political strength and overseas naval dominance are different forms of power.

Art and legitimacy

Mughal painting included miniatures. Architecture combined Persian-Islamic and Indian traditions. The Taj Mahal, commissioned by Shah Jahan as a mausoleum, demonstrated imperial wealth and cultural synthesis. The Peacock Throne similarly represented royal magnificence.

Decline

Aurangzeb’s prolonged military campaigns, particularly in the south, strained finances. Religious restrictions and renewed jizya contributed to opposition, while political competition and rebellions weakened central authority. Instability after his death in 1707 created opportunities for regional rulers and European companies.

The British East India Company first pursued commercial interests and later gained political and territorial power. Do not say that it directly ruled most of India for the entire two centuries of its existence.

Ready-to-use comparison: “Akbar used religious accommodation to expand cooperation with Hindu elites. Aurangzeb’s stricter policies created additional grievances, while costly warfare intensified financial pressure. Mughal decline therefore had political, religious, and economic causes.”

PPT evidence: Mughal Empire Notes, slides 3–11, especially the comparison on slide 9.

5. Ming Dynasty 明朝

Dates: 1368–1644. Identity: Restoration of Han Chinese rule after the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty.

Founders and central government

Hongwu / Zhu Yuanzhang founded the dynasty in 1368. He strengthened imperial authority and promoted agricultural recovery. The government emphasized Confucian learning and the civil service examination tradition.

Yongle / Zhu Di strengthened the imperial capital at Beijing, associated his rule with the Forbidden City, and sponsored Zheng He’s voyages. He was Hongwu’s son, but was not the emperor immediately after Hongwu.

Confucian bureaucracy and society

  • The emperor governed through officials educated in Confucian texts.
  • The Hanlin Academy was an elite scholarly institution connected with imperial learning and official writing.
  • Meritocracy means advancement based on ability or achievement. Examinations offered a route into office, but wealth, education, gender, and family resources limited access.
  • The ideal hierarchy 士农工商 placed scholars above farmers, artisans, and merchants. Actual wealth did not always match this ranking: merchants could be rich despite lower formal prestige.
  • Neo-Confucianism stressed moral cultivation and social order. The PPT contrasts established scholarly traditions with thinkers such as Wang Yangming who emphasized moral knowledge.

Zheng He and overseas voyages

Zheng He, a Muslim eunuch admiral, led seven voyages between 1405 and 1433 through Indian Ocean networks.

Reasons: Display imperial power, develop diplomatic and tributary relations, obtain goods and knowledge, and extend Chinese influence. These were state-sponsored expeditions. They differed from later European projects that established extensive overseas colonial empires.

Why they ended: High costs, changing court priorities, official opposition, and concern over northern threats reduced support.

Effects: China ended this particular program of large official voyages and shifted resources toward other priorities. Commercial exchange continued, so the end of the voyages should not be described as the end of all Chinese foreign trade.

Economy, environment, and restrictions

The Grand Canal connected northern and southern China and helped move grain toward the capital. Irrigation and agricultural recovery supported population and revenue. Silk and porcelain were important products.

Haijin, or maritime prohibitions, restricted private maritime activity at different times. Sinocentrism framed China as the center of civilization. The PPT uses “isolationism,” but selective restriction and regulated foreign contact are more precise than “China had no outside contact.”

Matteo Ricci, a Jesuit missionary, illustrates continuing exchange in religion, mathematics, and science.

Decline

Economic stress, food shortages, rebellion, and weakening authority destabilized the dynasty. Rebels captured Beijing in 1644, and Manchu forces entered during the crisis. The Qing takeover involved warfare and alliances and continued beyond that single year.

Ready-to-use answer: “Ming rulers restored Confucian institutions and strengthened the emperor after Mongol rule. Although Yongle sponsored major voyages, later governments prioritized domestic and northern security concerns, restricting some maritime activities while retaining other forms of exchange.”

PPT evidence: Ming and Qing, slides 2–15.

6. Qing Dynasty 清朝

Dates for rule over China: 1644–1912. The PPT also uses 1911, the year of the revolution. Ruling group: Manchus from the northeast.

Rise

Manchu military organization, including the Banner System, gave the Qing a foundation for conquest. Ming rebellions and political breakdown created an opportunity. Alliances with Chinese elites and soldiers also helped the conquest and consolidation of rule.

Nurhaci was a foundational Manchu leader associated with banner organization. Kangxi consolidated the state and expanded its power. Yongzheng and Qianlong continued imperial administration and expansion.

Continuity with earlier Chinese dynasties

The Qing retained the emperor, Confucian ideals, civil service examinations, scholar-officials, and an agricultural tax base. These institutions helped Manchu rulers govern a predominantly Han population and claim legitimate imperial authority.

Change and Manchu identity

The Manchus preserved a distinct ruling identity and maintained privileged political and military positions. Bannermen belonged to banner organizations, which included Manchu, Mongol, and Han components. The required queue hairstyle became a visible sign of submission to Qing authority for many male subjects.

Continuity-and-change thesis: “The Qing largely continued earlier Chinese systems of government through Confucian bureaucracy and examinations, while Manchu privilege, banner organization, and the queue requirement changed the relationship between the ruling elite and the population.”

Economy and foreign relations

Agricultural expansion and American crops such as maize and sweet potatoes supported population growth. Chinese goods remained important in global trade. Expansion of the state also increased demands on administration and resources.

The Canton System, introduced in 1757, concentrated regulated Western maritime trade at Guangzhou. This belongs after the central 1450–1750 period but appears in your PPT.

Tribute and diplomatic rituals such as kowtow expressed hierarchy in foreign relations. Tributary relations did not automatically mean that another country was directly annexed and governed as a Chinese province.

Later pressures: extension material in the PPT

The Opium Wars, unequal treaties, and major rebellions belong mainly to the nineteenth century. The First Opium War (1839–1842) ended with the Treaty of Nanjing, including the cession of Hong Kong and opening of treaty ports. This expanded foreign access rather than simply making China more closed.

The PPT also lists the Taiping Rebellion and Boxer Rebellion. Review them as later Qing context if your teacher includes the entire deck, but keep them separate from events in 1450–1750.

PPT evidence: Ming and Qing, slides 3–10 and 16–20.

7. Swahili City-States 斯瓦希里城邦

Location: East African coast. PPT focus: c. 1400–1600. Political form: Independent trading city-states, rather than one unified empire.

Politics and society

Each city-state had its own ruler, sometimes a Muslim sultan. States competed over commerce and regional influence. Wealthy merchants and officials often practiced Islam and held high status, while commoners had less power.

Cultural development

Bantu-speaking populations formed the local foundation of Swahili civilization. Contacts with Arab, Persian, and Indian merchants contributed to cultural exchange.

Swahili is a Bantu language with many Arabic loanwords. It served as a lingua franca, helping people with different first languages conduct trade. Islam spread through commercial and social connections. Qadis applied Islamic law in their work as judges.

Bantu migrations spread languages and knowledge of farming and ironworking across large parts of Africa over a long period. This process preceded the PPT’s 1400–1600 focus.

Indian Ocean trade and environment

Exports included gold, ivory, and valuable woods. Cities connected the African interior, including networks linked with Great Zimbabwe, to Arabia, India, and markets reaching China. Enslaved people also entered Indian Ocean trading networks.

Monsoon winds reverse seasonally. Sailors planned outward and return journeys around these predictable patterns. Shipbuilding, navigation, lateen sails, and steering technology supported exchange. The exact wind direction varies by location, so learn the seasonal reversal rather than a universal east/west rule.

Change over time

Trade increased merchant wealth and strengthened Islamic cultural connections. Commercial growth encouraged both cooperation across the ocean and competition between coastal states. European maritime expansion later challenged existing trading arrangements, but detailed Portuguese conquest is not covered in this downloaded Swahili deck.

Ready-to-use answer: “Indian Ocean trade strengthened Swahili merchant elites and encouraged the spread of Islam. Swahili became a shared trading language, while knowledge of seasonal monsoons made regular long-distance voyages possible.”

PPT evidence: Swahili City-States (1), slides 3–10.

8. Aztec / Mexica Empire 阿兹特克帝国

Location: Central Mexico. Capital: Tenochtitlan, on an island in Lake Texcoco. Source basis: Unit 2 Quizlet and conquest sections of the Spanish PPT. No separate Aztec PPT was included among the eight downloads.

Government, economy, and religion

  • The ruler, called the Great Speaker, held political and religious authority.
  • Tribute from conquered communities supported imperial power. Resentment of imperial demands created potential allies for Spain.
  • Chinampas were productive raised fields in shallow wetland areas. The common phrase “floating gardens” is misleading if taken literally.
  • Pochteca were specialized long-distance merchants who also gathered information.
  • Obsidian was used for sharp cutting edges and weapons. Turquoise was a valued ornamental material.
  • Huitzilopochtli, associated with war and the sun, was a major deity. Human sacrifice formed part of Mexica religious practice and imperial authority.

Conquest

Hernán Cortés used alliances with Indigenous opponents of Mexica power, including Tlaxcalans. Spanish weapons and horses mattered, but Spanish victory depended heavily on Indigenous participation. Smallpox and other disruptions weakened resistance. Tenochtitlan fell in 1521.

Moctezuma II was the Mexica ruler during the initial encounter and died in 1520 while in Spanish custody. Other leaders continued the defense after his death.

Ready-to-use answer: “The Spanish conquest of the Mexica resulted from the interaction of Indigenous alliances, epidemic disease, and military force. Resentment of Mexica rule supplied Cortés with allies and resources that his small Spanish force could not have provided alone.”

PPT evidence: Spanish Empire, slides 9 and 25; Unit 2 Quizlet.

9. Inca Empire 印加帝国

Location: Andes Mountains in western South America. Capital: Cuzco/Cusco. Important places: Machu Picchu and Lake Titicaca.

Rise, government, and religion

Pachacuti greatly expanded the state from 1438. The Sapa Inca ruled through a centralized administration and claimed a special relationship with Inti, the sun god.

The ayllu, a community based on related families and shared obligations, formed a basic unit of society. The hierarchy placed the emperor and royal family above nobles and commoners.

Religion was polytheistic and included sacred natural places. The ruler’s association with Inti helped legitimize political authority.

Labor, agriculture, and redistribution

The mit’a was a rotational labor obligation. Workers contributed to state farming, crafts, roads, irrigation, and other projects. The state collected and redistributed resources through administrative systems and storehouses.

Terrace farming created more usable farmland on steep slopes. Irrigation and crops adapted to mountain environments supported the population. Compared with the Aztecs, the PPT emphasizes centralized provision and less reliance on extensive commercial markets.

Communication and technology

  • Qhapaq Ñan: extensive road network connecting regions of the empire. Quizlet calls it “Carpa Nan.”
  • Chasquis: relay messengers who carried information over long distances.
  • Quipu/khipu: knotted-cord record-keeping devices used for quantities and administrative information.
  • Roads, bridges, officials, and record keeping helped coordinate labor and maintain authority across difficult terrain.

The PPT and Quizlet give conflicting road lengths. Learn the function first. UNESCO describes a network covering more than 30,000 km. See the correction section.

Conquest and resistance

A succession struggle between Atahualpa and Huáscar, together with epidemic disease, weakened the empire before the Spanish conquest. Francisco Pizarro captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532; the Spanish executed him in 1533. Capture of the ruler destabilized a highly centralized state. Indigenous alliances also helped Spanish forces.

Inca resistance continued after the first Spanish victories. “Atahualpa was the last Inca ruler” is too broad because later Inca rulers and resistance states existed.

Ready-to-use answer: “The Inca maintained power through labor obligations, agricultural adaptation, and a coordinated road and record-keeping system. Civil war and disease weakened this structure, allowing Pizarro to exploit political division and the capture of Atahualpa.”

PPT evidence: Inca, slides 3–13; Spanish Empire, slides 10 and 26.

10. Taíno Society 泰诺社会

Location: Caribbean, including Hispaniola and other islands. This was not one centralized empire.

Caciques were chiefs. Zemís could refer to spiritual beings and objects associated with them. Conucos were raised planting beds. Manioc/cassava was an important crop.

Columbus and colonization

In 1492, Columbus reached the Caribbean while searching for a westward route to Asia. Ferdinand and Isabella sponsored his voyage. He encountered Taíno communities and later visited Hispaniola.

Colonization brought enslavement, demands for labor, land seizure, violence, disease, and disruption of local authority. These pressures caused catastrophic population decline and loss of political independence.

Avoid an extinction claim: Taíno descendants and cultural legacies survive today. The claim in the PPT that Caribbean Indigenous people were simply “gone in 50 years” is too absolute.

Ready-to-use answer: “Spanish colonization devastated Taíno communities through the combined effects of disease, coerced labor, violence, and dispossession. These changes undermined their population and political autonomy, while descendants and elements of Taíno culture survived.”

PPT evidence: Spanish Empire, slides 6–7, 14–15, 22, and 24.

11. Spanish Empire 西班牙帝国

Main regions in these materials: Caribbean, Mexico, Central and South America, and the Philippines.

Motives and expansion

Economic motives included access to Asian goods, precious metals, land, and profitable crops. Religious motives included spreading Catholicism. Monarchs and conquistadors also sought power and prestige. The PPT connects exploration with the religious and military culture of the Reconquista, completed with the conquest of Granada in 1492.

People to distinguish: Columbus = Caribbean encounter; Cortés = Mexica conquest; Pizarro = Inca conquest.

Rivalry with Portugal

The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided Spanish and Portuguese claims using an Atlantic demarcation line. The Treaty of Zaragoza/Saragossa (1529) addressed rivalry around Asian spice routes and claims in the eastern hemisphere. These were European agreements and did not represent consent from the peoples whose territories they claimed.

Magellan’s expedition left in 1519. Magellan died in the Philippines in 1521. Elcano brought the surviving ship home in 1522, completing the circumnavigation. Spanish colonization of the Philippines developed from 1565, rather than being completed by Magellan’s initial arrival.

Colonial government

Viceroys represented the monarch. Distance and slow communication gave colonial officials considerable practical power. Colonial cities became centers of government, religion, and economic organization.

Labor systems: know the differences

System Basic meaning Key distinction
Encomienda Grant of rights to receive Indigenous tribute, sometimes including labor, with stated duties of protection and Christian instruction A grant over tribute obligations, not simply ownership of all land and people
Repartimiento Required labor drafts for a limited period, nominally with wages Workers remained legally free but labor was coerced
Colonial mit’a Spanish adaptation of Andean labor drafts, especially for mining Redirected an existing labor institution toward colonial extraction
Hacienda Large landed estate producing agricultural goods Describes an estate and rural economic arrangement, not one identical legal labor status
Chattel slavery People treated as property, often with hereditary enslaved status Hereditary property status differs from temporary labor drafts

At Potosí, silver production enriched Spanish interests and relied heavily on coerced labor. The continuity was the use of labor obligations. The change was their redirection toward Spanish colonial extraction, with severe consequences for workers.

Casta hierarchy

Peninsulares were Spaniards born in Spain. Creoles/criollos were people of Spanish ancestry born in the Americas. Mestizo referred to mixed European and Indigenous ancestry. Mulatto, a historical colonial term, referred to mixed European and African ancestry.

Ancestry, birthplace, legal status, wealth, and reputation all affected position. Peninsulares generally dominated the highest offices, while Indigenous people and enslaved Africans faced substantial exploitation and discrimination. The familiar classroom ladder is a simplified model, not a perfectly fixed ranking applied identically everywhere.

Casta paintings represented and reinforced ideas about ancestry and hierarchy. They reveal colonial social assumptions rather than providing neutral scientific categories.

Religion, cultural change, and resistance

Missionaries spread Catholicism. Conversion could involve persuasion, pressure, and coercion. Religious syncretism combined traditions:

  • Virgin of Guadalupe: Catholic devotion associated with an Indigenous apparition tradition involving Juan Diego.
  • Día de los Muertos: Indigenous traditions and Catholic commemorations of the dead.
  • Vodou: developed in Haiti through African religious traditions and Catholic influences. This is a wider Atlantic example, not an exclusively Spanish colonial religion.

The Pueblo Revolt (1680) challenged Spanish rule and religious coercion in New Mexico. The Spanish returned in 1692. The Túpac Amaru II rebellion (1780), also in the PPT, belongs to a later period and demonstrates continuing Indigenous resistance.

Ready-to-use answer: “Spanish colonization reorganized society around resource extraction and unequal colonial status. Coerced labor supplied mines and estates, while the casta hierarchy associated ancestry and birthplace with power. Catholic conversion transformed religious life, but Indigenous resistance and syncretism limited complete cultural replacement.”

PPT evidence: Spanish Empire, slides 3–21 and 24–30.

12. Other States and Societies in Quizlet

These shorter sections cover Quizlet material for which no dedicated PPT was downloaded.

Portugal 葡萄牙

Prince Henry the Navigator supported maritime exploration. Bartolomeu Dias rounded southern Africa in 1488. Vasco da Gama reached India by sea in 1498, establishing a direct European maritime route around Africa.

The main pattern was using ocean navigation to access trade routes and valuable goods. Learn the order Henry’s sponsorship → Dias around Africa → da Gama to India. Lateen sails, astrolabes, and stern-post rudders supported navigation and ship handling, although these technologies had histories that preceded European exploration.

English / British colonies 英国殖民地

  • Jamestown (1607): first permanent English settlement in North America.
  • Pilgrims / Plymouth (1620): the Pilgrims were Separatists who had broken with the Church of England.
  • Puritans / Massachusetts Bay: sought to reform English Christianity. Distinguish their project from the Pilgrims’ separation.
  • King Philip’s War (1675–1676): conflict involving New England colonists and Indigenous peoples, including forces led by Metacom, also called King Philip. Land encroachment was a major source of tension.
  • French and Indian War (1754–1763): North American conflict connected to the global Seven Years’ War. Britain defeated France, changing the balance of colonial power. This extends beyond 1750.

French colonial networks 法国殖民贸易网络

The fur trade linked French traders with Indigenous communities. Trade relationships and alliances were important to French colonial influence. Indigenous peoples pursued their own interests in these relationships and were not merely passive partners.

Tokugawa Japan 德川日本

Sakoku refers to restrictions on foreign relations and trade under the Tokugawa shogunate. It belongs to Japan, while haijin belongs to Chinese maritime prohibitions. Sakoku did not eliminate every foreign contact or every permitted trading relationship.

Other Indigenous societies 美洲其他原住民社会

  • Haudenosaunee / Iroquois Confederacy: alliance of Indigenous nations in northeastern North America. Originally five nations, later six with the Tuscarora. Clan mothers held important political roles. “Matrilineal” describes descent through the mother’s line more precisely than simply calling the entire society a “matriarchy.”
  • Pueblo peoples / “Oasisans” in Quizlet: diverse Southwestern communities associated with settled agriculture, including maize, beans, and squash. “Oasisans” is a broad regional classroom label, not one unified empire.
  • Cahokia: large Mississippian urban and ceremonial center near present-day St. Louis. It demonstrates major Indigenous urban development before European colonization.
  • Mayapán: important late Postclassic Maya city. Maya civilization consisted of multiple political centers rather than one continuous unified empire.

13. Connections Across Empires

Columbian Exchange 哥伦布大交换

The movement of crops, animals, people, and pathogens between the Americas and Afro-Eurasia following sustained Atlantic contact.

Direction Examples Effects
Americas to Afro-Eurasia Maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, cacao New food supplies and crops supported population and economic changes
Afro-Eurasia to Americas Horses, cattle, pigs, wheat, sugarcane Changed agriculture, transport, diets, land use, and labor demand
Pathogens to Americas Smallpox, measles, influenza Epidemics weakened communities, leadership, and resistance
Forced movement of people Enslaved Africans transported to the Americas Expanded coerced labor and African cultural influences under violent conditions

The Great Dying refers to catastrophic Indigenous population losses. Disease interacted with war, displacement, hunger, and exploitation. The effects were highly unequal: do not turn an increase in food supplies elsewhere into a claim of immediate population growth everywhere in the Americas.

Atlantic economy and slavery

Mercantilism connected national power with wealth, favorable trade, and colonial resources. Joint-stock companies pooled investment and distributed commercial risk.

Plantations producing sugar, tobacco, and other export crops demanded large labor forces. The Atlantic slave trade forcibly transported Africans to the Americas. The Middle Passage was the Atlantic crossing within that system. Chattel slavery made enslaved status a form of property and often hereditary.

The “triangle trade” model links European manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and American commodities. Actual Atlantic voyages followed many routes, so not every ship completed a literal triangle.

Silver from American mines entered international exchange and helped connect the Americas, Europe, and Asian markets, including China. This is an example of economic integration across the land-based/maritime unit boundary.

Islam and governance

Sunni and Shiʿa traditions differ in their understandings of legitimate succession after Muhammad and developed distinct institutions and traditions. Both are branches of Islam.

The Quizlet also includes shahadah (profession of faith), salah (prayer), sawm (Ramadan fasting), and hajj (pilgrimage). Zakat, obligatory almsgiving, completes the Five Pillars even though it is not a separate entry in these sets. Hadith are reports about Muhammad’s words and actions. Sufism emphasizes spiritual discipline and closeness to God.

Religion could legitimize rulers, support learning, build commercial trust, and organize communities. Coercion and religious inequality could also create resentment or conflict.

Two ideas that often produce stronger answers

Toleration is a governing strategy. Explain how accommodation gains cooperation, reduces resistance, or makes taxation easier. Also recognize that accommodation may coexist with hierarchy.

Technology changes what a ruler can do. Cannons at Constantinople, firearms at Chaldiran and Tondibi, camel saddles in the Sahara, and roads in the Andes worked through different mechanisms. Identify the mechanism rather than simply saying “technology helped.”

15. Practice Questions and Answer Outlines

Short answers

  1. How did geography support Songhai’s power? Discuss the Niger region, gold-salt exchange, camel transport, route protection, and tax revenue.
  2. How did Islam affect Songhai society? Explain elite legitimacy, scholarly centers, and wider diplomatic/commercial connections, while acknowledging local religious continuity.
  3. Were Ottoman institutions exploitation or pragmatic governance? Use devshirme’s coercion, opportunities for service, millet autonomy, and jizya inequality. A reasoned answer can recognize both.
  4. Why did the Ottomans defeat the Safavids at Chaldiran? Explain the military advantage of artillery and firearms, rather than treating religious identity itself as a battlefield weapon.
  5. How did Akbar increase support for Mughal rule? Connect Hindu participation, marriage alliances, and tax/religious accommodation with cooperation from local elites.
  6. What is the difference between a mansabdar and a zamindar? Imperial rank and service versus local landholding/intermediary authority and revenue collection.
  7. Why did the Ming stop Zheng He’s voyages? Explain cost, court priorities, and northern security concerns. Distinguish the end of official voyages from the end of all trade.
  8. How did Qing rulers combine continuity and change? Confucian bureaucracy and examinations continued, while Manchu identity and banners preserved a distinct ruling elite.
  9. How did trade change Swahili society? Explain merchant status, Islam, lingua franca, and cultural exchange.
  10. How did the Inca rule across mountains? Link terraces and irrigation with food production, mit’a with public works, and roads/chasquis/quipu with coordination.
  11. Why could small Spanish forces defeat large American states? Include large Indigenous allied forces, disease, existing divisions, and military force. Avoid assuming only a few Spaniards fought.
  12. How did Spanish colonization affect social structure? Connect extraction with coerced labor and ancestry/birthplace with unequal power.
  13. Give two examples of syncretism and explain what blended. Use Guadalupe or Day of the Dead for Catholic and Indigenous influences, and Vodou for African and Catholic influences.
  14. How did the Columbian Exchange have unequal effects? Contrast new food supplies with epidemic death, enslavement, dispossession, and changing land use.

Essay-ready theses

Religious policy: “Early modern rulers used religion to strengthen legitimacy and organize diverse populations, but their strategies differed: Ottoman communal autonomy and Akbar’s accommodation broadened cooperation, while Safavid religious enforcement created a distinctive identity alongside serious conflict.”

American conquests: “Spanish conquest succeeded through the interaction of epidemic disease, existing political divisions, and military alliances. Although weapons mattered in both Mexico and the Andes, anti-Mexica coalitions and the Inca succession war created different opportunities for Spanish intervention.”

Qing continuity: “The Qing continued the Confucian bureaucratic foundations of earlier dynasties to a considerable extent, while preserving Manchu privilege through banner organization and visible markers of submission.”

Economic development and hierarchy: “Expanding trade and colonial extraction strengthened unequal social structures by enriching merchants and imperial elites while intensifying demands on labor. Swahili merchant status and Spanish colonial labor and casta arrangements illustrate different forms of this relationship.”

Paragraph structure

Claim: State a specific historical argument. Evidence: Name a policy, person, event, or institution. Explanation: Show how the evidence supports the claim.

Example: “Akbar strengthened Mughal rule through religious accommodation. He abolished jizya and included Hindu elites in government. These policies reduced some barriers between the Muslim court and its Hindu subjects and gave influential local groups reasons to cooperate with the empire.”

Cause vocabulary: Your Songhai PPT uses “primary cause” for a direct or especially important cause and “secondary cause” for a contributing background factor. In an essay, identify both immediate triggers and longer-term conditions, then explain their relative importance. Do not confuse these cause labels with primary sources and secondary sources.

17. Corrections and Common Traps

  1. Nadir Shah was not a Safavid king. He replaced Safavid rule with the Afsharid dynasty in 1736. The 1722 capture of Isfahan marks the collapse of effective Safavid power, while the dynasty’s final displacement occurred later. Encyclopaedia Iranica
  2. Pizarro conquered the Inca; Cortés conquered the Mexica. Spanish PPT slide 10 mistakenly switches names. The same deck’s slide 26 correctly identifies Pizarro.
  3. Magellan died in 1521; the circumnavigation ended in 1522. Elcano completed the return. Spanish PPT slide 4 gives the wrong death year. National Historical Commission of the Philippines
  4. Qing rule ended in 1912. The 1911 revolution explains why some art-historical and classroom periodizations end at 1911. Conquest of China also did not finish entirely in 1644. Columbia University, Asia for Educators
  5. Trade restrictions did not mean complete isolation. The Opium War’s settlement opened additional ports and expanded foreign access. The deck’s suggestion that the wars simply intensified isolation is misleading. Columbia University’s Opium War guide
  6. Chinampas were constructed raised fields in wetlands. They did not drift around as floating rafts. UN Food and Agriculture Organization
  7. Inca road lengths conflict in your sources. The PPT says 2,500 miles and Quizlet says 25,000 miles. UNESCO describes the Qhapaq Ñan network as more than 30,000 km. Remember its administrative and communication functions first. UNESCO
  8. Taíno survival matters. Catastrophic demographic collapse does not mean that all Taíno people and traditions vanished. Smithsonian
  9. Mansabdars and zamindars are different. Use Mughal PPT slide 9, which is more precise than the simplified Quizlet definitions.
  10. Tributary relations are different from annexation. The Quizlet’s Kangxi entry incorrectly groups tributary regions and territories under direct Qing rule. Do not memorize its entire territorial list as conquests by one emperor.
  11. An empire can use powerful armies without controlling an entire ocean. This is particularly important for Mughal Indian Ocean trade.
  12. “Guns, Germs, and Steel” is a mnemonic. An explanation of conquest must also include Indigenous alliances, political divisions, and resistance.
  13. Source spellings: Jannisaries → Janissaries; Constanstinople → Constantinople; Auranzeb → Aurangzeb; qiupu → quipu; Huadenosaunee → Haudenosaunee; Mercantalism → mercantilism; Columbian Excahnge → Columbian Exchange; Carpa Nan → Qhapaq Ñan. Shia, Shiʿa, and Shiite refer to the same branch in this vocabulary context.
  14. Avoid uncertain trivia. Do not prioritize the deck’s claims about a “first machine gun,” the exact inventor of mahjong, or “Queen Victoria II.” These are outside the main learning objectives and include questionable or mistaken wording.
